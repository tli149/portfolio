Architectural education should stay in contact with the civic life it designs for. The project challenges an elitist, top-down pedagogy that keeps isolated abstractions and speculation apart from the disciplines and networks of that life.

Placing the intervention inside an architecture school turns the critique back on its own spaces. A living archive in its atrium, or agora, and a campus corridor for public access challenge the habit of treating the public as a static scenario. The politics of difference become a reason to question and inform how communities are designed for.

Two walls of knowledge replace the original barriers between professional spaces and the public atrium. The move makes that educational critique a spatial one.

The two glass walls create a large vertical space that students and faculty encounter on entering school programs. They extend below the building into a large auditorium beneath a campus corridor, which gives the public access to the archive and academic discussion. Each pairs archive storage with openable, double-layered glass panels where students and faculty insert drawings for display toward both the atrium and the wall interior. The storage backs serve fourth-floor studio pin-ups, a screen for the Selignmen Auditorium on the first and second floors, and exhibitions in the marble room. Archive displays, lectures, studio critiques, and work or study at foldable glass desks before the displayed masterpieces bring archives out of limited-access storehouses and into constant contact with students, the building, and the public.
