**Afterlife Treatment of Modern Architecture.** The thesis accepts weathering as part of architecture's life. A first use becomes one phase in a longer relationship with a changing context.

Modern buildings usually end in demolition when they no longer serve their owners, or preservation at the "perfect" moment just after construction. Both reject the passing of time. Demolition also makes architecture disposable, with construction waste accounting for a large share of all waste and waste-related carbon emissions.

The proposed building slowly degenerates while interacting with its surroundings. It shifts from architecture for humans to architecture for the local ecosystem, continuing beyond one program without being torn down.

On Inujima, a small island in the Seto Inland Sea, this change runs from the present to 2300. Existing houses first become art studios, then habitats for marine species as sea levels rise. The Buildings as Material Banks (BAMB) model guides reversible connections so components can be removed and reused. Mortar and oyster-shell concrete let surfaces survive underwater and become a base for marine life. Weathering mediates between spatial experience and material production.
