**Afterlife Treatment of Modern Architecture.** How does architecture end its life? Modern buildings are usually either demolished once they no longer serve their owners, or preserved in the "perfect" moment right after construction. Both attitudes reject the passing of time, and demolition turns architecture into a disposable product: construction waste makes up a large share of all waste and its carbon emissions.

This thesis proposes architecture that accepts weathering. Instead of being consumed for one program and then torn down, a building slowly degenerates while interacting with its changing context, and in doing so shifts from architecture for humans to architecture for the local ecosystem.

The project is set on Inujima, a small island in the Seto Inland Sea, from the present to 2300. Existing houses first become art studios, then, as the sea level rises, habitats for marine species. Following the Buildings as Material Banks (BAMB) model, components are joined reversibly so they can be removed and reused. Mortar and oyster-shell concrete let the surfaces survive underwater and become a base for marine life. Weathering acts as the mediator between spatial experience and material production.
